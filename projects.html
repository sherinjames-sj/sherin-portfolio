🛗 Predictive Maintenance for Elevators

Can sensor data tell you an elevator is heading for trouble before it actually breaks? For my MSc dissertation, I investigated predictive maintenance using real-world IoT time-series data from 100 elevator units, 21 raw sensor readings per operating cycle (vibration, temperature, pressure and more), with one cycle representing one day of operation.

I compared LSTM, Random Forest, CNN, ARIMA and XGBoost, then built two purpose-built LSTM models: one to classify which elevators were at risk of failure, and one to estimate their Remaining Useful Life (RUL) in days.

Data

Each of the 100 elevator units contributed daily sensor readings across its operating life. I engineered the 21 raw sensor readings into 25 features per cycle, then windowed the data into overlapping 60-cycle sequences, producing 14,631 training sequences with the shape (14,631 × 60 × 25).

🏷️ Classification model

  • LSTM, 128 + 64 units, dropout, sigmoid output
  • 128,321 trainable parameters
  • Task: is this elevator at risk of failure?

📉 Regression model

  • LSTM, 100 + 50 units, dropout, linear output
  • 80,651 trainable parameters
  • Task: how many days of useful life are left?

The model achieved approximately 95% accuracy, with an F1 score of 0.940 and AUC of 0.957 for failure classification. RUL prediction achieved an MAE of approximately 12.97 days.

What the sensors showed

🔍 Two findings stood out while exploring the raw sensor traces: sensors S4 and S21 spiked abnormally in the lead-up to Elevator Unit 5's breakdown, and sensor S7 showed a consistent dip before Elevator Unit 10 failed. Neither pattern was obvious from a single reading, only visible once I plotted the sequences over time, which is a big part of why the sequence-based LSTM approach made sense here.

What I learned

  • 🧩Most of the actual work was in the messy middle: cleaning sensor data, deciding how to window sequences, and choosing evaluation metrics that meant something for maintenance decisions, not just accuracy for its own sake.
  • ⚖️Classification and regression needed different architectures and different framings of the same underlying data. Treating them identically would have hurt both.
  • 🔬The LSTM is good at pattern recognition, but it can't tell you why a sensor spiked, only that it did. That gap between correlation and explanation is something I'd want to dig into further with more causal, interpretable methods.

💌 Purrfect Match

A CV × job-fit copilot I built because job hunting needed help.

I got tired of AI resume tools that confidently tell people they have skills they absolutely do not have.

So I built my own.

Paste in a CV and job description and Purrfect Match analyses the genuine overlap, highlights what's missing, and helps rewrite existing experience to better match the language of the role, without inventing experience.

How it came together

Designed around one rule: never tell someone they're qualified for something they're not. Built as a single-page tool with Claude doing the actual CV-to-job comparison. Tested against my own CV and real job postings until the matching felt honest rather than flattering. Evaluated and iterated from there, which is how it grew from a one-trick matcher into the small toolkit below.

Then I kept adding things.

What it grew into

🐾 Whiskers & Biscuit

An AI chat companion for wording help, pep talks and job-hunt questions.

🎯 Interview Predictor

Generates likely interview questions from the actual job posting.

✉️ Cover Letter Starter

Creates a starting point based on the user's real experience.

📓 Job Hunt Journal

Private browser-based notes stored locally with localStorage.

🎮 Need a break?

Snake and Flappy Cat, built entirely in vanilla JavaScript and Canvas.

Under the hood

The matching system uses Claude to return structured JSON, allowing the UI to consume fields directly rather than trying to parse a giant block of generated text.

The chat, interview predictor and cover-letter tools use separate stateless calls with the CV and job description supplied as context.

And the games? Absolutely no AI. Just JavaScript, Canvas and questionable amounts of procrastination.

🐀 Live AI tool · built solo · for fun

🧠 Brainrot-O-Meter

An intentionally silly experiment in applying LLMs to subjective, informal human language.

Paste some text and Claude gives it a brainrot score out of 100, assigns it a completely unnecessary tier name, delivers a short roast, and, if you're brave, translates it into maximum Gen Alpha.

I built this one in an afternoon to experiment with something different from the CV tool: getting an LLM to make consistent, structured judgments about something as fuzzy and subjective as internet slang.

Structured LLM outputs, streaming responses, and deliberately ridiculous UX.

What I was playing with

🧠 Structured JSON

Claude returns a predictable schema containing the score, tier, roast and recovery tip.

⚡ Streaming

The brainrot translation appears token by token instead of arriving as one giant response.

🎨 Playful UX

A completely different visual language from the rest of this portfolio.

Both interactions are stateless. New text means a new diagnosis. Nothing is remembered between requests. Small and silly on the surface, but the underlying problem, getting consistent structured judgments out of an LLM on subjective input, shows up in far more serious applications too.

🧪 Volunteer AI Testing · Healthcare AI

🧪 AI Testing & Red-Teaming

Volunteering to break someone else's AI, on purpose, before their users could.

A few months ago I connected with someone on LinkedIn who was building an AI chatbot for the Australian healthcare space. I volunteered to test the chatbot's limits, deliberately probing ambiguous inputs, edge cases, unexpected behaviour and failure conditions.

It fell apart a couple of times. Which was, inconveniently, exactly what I was looking for.

What started as "let me see if I can break this" turned into hands-on AI testing and red-teaming.

What I actually tested

❓ Ambiguous inputs

Vague or incomplete questions the bot had to interpret.

🌪️ Edge cases

Unusual phrasing and scenarios outside the obvious happy path.

🙃 Unexpected behaviour

How the bot handled users who didn't follow the expected flow.

🔁 Inconsistent responses

Whether the same question got the same answer twice.

🧱 Robustness & failure conditions

Where and how the bot broke, and what it did once it had.

🗣️ Response quality

Whether answers were accurate, appropriate and safe for a healthcare context.

I'm not a certified security red-teamer and this wasn't a paid or formal engagement, just genuine curiosity about where AI systems break, applied to a real bot with real stakes. It turned into a properly useful crash course in probing model behaviour, finding edge cases, and figuring out where a bot needs to be more robust before real users rely on it. The full story is also in Beyond Work.
